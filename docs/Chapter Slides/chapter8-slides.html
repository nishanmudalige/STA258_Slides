8.1 Why ANOVA?

Purpose

Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) is used to compare several population means at once.

Instead of running many separate two-sample tests, ANOVA begins with one global test.

Question

Do all groups have the same population mean?

\[ k \ge 3 \]

groups are being compared.

Null hypothesis

The population means are all equal:

\[ H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2=\cdots=\mu_k \]

Alternative hypothesis

At least one population mean is different:

\[ H_a:\text{not all }\mu_i\text{ are equal} \]

Key idea

ANOVA tells us whether there is evidence that at least one mean differs.

If ANOVA rejects (H_0), then pairwise comparisons can ask which means differ.

One-Factor ANOVA Model

Textbook model

For observation \(j\) in group \(i\):

\[ Y_{ij}=\mu+\tau_i+\epsilon_{ij}, \qquad \epsilon_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma^2), \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{k}\tau_i=0 \]

Here, \(Y_{ij}\) is the response value for observation \(j\) in group \(i\). The constraint \(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tau_i=0\) makes \(\mu\) the overall population mean and uniquely defines the treatment effects.

Equivalent form

Since the population mean for group \(i\) is

\[ \mu_i=\mu+\tau_i, \]

the same model can be written as

\[ Y_{ij}=\mu_i+\epsilon_{ij}, \qquad \epsilon_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma^2) \]

Meaning of the notation

  • \(\mu\): overall population mean
  • \(\tau_i\): treatment effect for group \(i\)
  • \(\epsilon_{ij}\): random error for observation \(j\) in group \(i\)
  • \(\mu_i=\mu+\tau_i\): population mean of group \(i\)

Intuition

Each observation is modeled as

\[ \text{response} = \text{group mean} + \text{random error}. \]

So ANOVA asks whether all group means are equal:

\[ H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2=\cdots=\mu_k \]

or whether at least one group mean differs.

Data Structure and Notation

Data layout

Group 1 Group 2 \(\cdots\) Group \(k\)
\(X_{11}\) \(X_{21}\) \(\cdots\) \(X_{k1}\)
\(X_{12}\) \(X_{22}\) \(\cdots\) \(X_{k2}\)
\(\vdots\) \(\vdots\) \(\ddots\) \(\vdots\)
\(X_{1n_1}\) \(X_{2n_2}\) \(\cdots\) \(X_{kn_k}\)
\(\bar X_1\) \(\bar X_2\) \(\cdots\) \(\bar X_k\)
\(S_1^2\) \(S_2^2\) \(\cdots\) \(S_k^2\)
\(n_1\) \(n_2\) \(\cdots\) \(n_k\)

Notation

  • \(\bar X_i\): sample mean of group \(i\)
  • \(S_i^2\): sample variance of group \(i\)
  • \(n_i\): sample size of group \(i\)

Overall summaries

Total sample size:

\[ n=n_1+n_2+\cdots+n_k \]

Overall sample mean:

\[ \bar X = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i}X_{ij} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{k}n_i\bar X_i \]

ANOVA as a Prediction Problem

Main idea

ANOVA compares two prediction rules: one that ignores the group and one that uses the group.

Ignore group information

Predict every observation using the overall mean:

\[ \widehat X_{ij}=\bar X \]

Total prediction error:

\[ SSTotal= \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i} (X_{ij}-\bar X)^2 \]

All observations are treated as coming from one common center.

Use group information

Predict observation \(j\) in group \(i\) using its group mean:

\[ \widehat X_{ij}=\bar X_i \]

Remaining prediction error:

\[ SSE= \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i} (X_{ij}-\bar X_i)^2 \]

Each group is allowed to have its own center.

Improvement from using groups

The variation explained by group membership is the reduction in prediction error:

\[ SSTr=SSTotal-SSE \]

If this improvement is large compared with the within-group error, the ANOVA (F) statistic becomes large.

8.2 Sources of Variation

ANOVA works by splitting variation into two pieces: variation within groups and variation between groups.

Within groups: error variation

\[ SSE=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(n_i-1)S_i^2 \]

This is residual variation inside the groups.

Between groups: treatment variation

\[ SSTr=\sum_{i=1}^{k} n_i(\bar X_i-\bar X)^2 \]

This captures how group means differ from the overall mean.

The ANOVA \(F\) statistic becomes large when between-group variation is large relative to within-group error variation.

Total Variation and Decomposition

Total variation

Total variation measures how far all observations are from the overall mean.

\[ SSTotal= \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i} (X_{ij}-\bar X)^2 \]

What ANOVA compares

ANOVA compares variation between group centers to variation inside the groups.

\[ \text{between-group variation} \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad \text{within-group variation} \]

Decomposition identity

Total variation splits into treatment variation plus error variation:

\[ SSTotal=SSTr+SSE \]

Intuition

If group means are close together, \(SSTr\) is small.

If at least one group mean is far away, \(SSTr\) becomes larger.

Big picture

ANOVA asks whether the between-group variation is large compared with the within-group error variation.

8.3 The ANOVA Table

ANOVA table structure

Source df SS MS \(F^*\)
Treatment \(k-1\) \(SSTr\) \(MSTr\) \(MSTr/MSE\)
Error \(n-k\) \(SSE\) \(MSE\)
Total \(n-1\) \(SSTotal\)

\[ SSTotal=SSTr+SSE \]

\[ F^*=\frac{MSTr}{MSE} \]

The p-value comes from the right tail of the (F) distribution.

Mean square rule

A mean square is a sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom:

\[ MS=\frac{SS}{df} \]

So in one-way ANOVA:

\[ MSTr=\frac{SSTr}{k-1}, \qquad MSE=\frac{SSE}{n-k} \]

Degrees of freedom

The degrees of freedom split the same way as the sums of squares:

\[ n-1=(k-1)+(n-k) \]

Treatment variation uses (k-1) df, and error variation uses (n-k) df.

Test statistic and p-value

The ANOVA test statistic is

\[ F^*=\frac{MSTr}{MSE} \]

Under \(H_0\),

\[ F^*\sim F_{k-1,\;n-k} \]

The p-value is the right-tail area.

ANOVA Hypothesis Test

Hypotheses

The null hypothesis says all population means are equal:

\[ H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2=\cdots=\mu_k \]

The alternative says at least one population mean differs:

\[ H_a:\text{not all }\mu_i\text{ are equal} \]

Reference distribution

Under \(H_0\), the test statistic follows an \(F\) distribution:

\[ F^* \sim F_{k-1,\;n-k} \]

Numerator df:

\[ k-1 \]

Denominator df:

\[ n-k \]

Test statistic

The ANOVA test statistic is

\[ F^* = \frac{MSTr}{MSE} = \frac{SSTr/(k-1)}{SSE/(n-k)} \]

It compares between-group variation to within-group error variation.

P-value

The ANOVA p-value is the area to the right of \(F^*\).

\[ p\text{-value}=P(F_{k-1,\;n-k}\ge F^*) \]

Large \(F^*\) values give evidence that the group means differ more than expected from random error alone.

8.4 Assumptions of ANOVA

Assumptions

  • Observations are independent.
  • Error terms, or residuals, are Normal.
  • All groups have the same population variance:

\[ \sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2=\cdots=\sigma_k^2=\sigma^2. \]

Common variance estimate

The common variance is estimated using the Mean Squared Error from ANOVA:

\[ \hat{\sigma}^2=MSE \]

What If ANOVA Rejects?

Global ANOVA result

ANOVA tests whether all population means are equal:

\[ H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2=\cdots=\mu_k \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad H_a:\text{at least one mean is different} \]

Important limitation

Rejecting \(H_0\) tells us that not all means are equal.

It does not tell us which specific groups differ.

Follow-up question

After a significant ANOVA result, we ask:

\[ \text{Which means are different?} \]

Pairwise comparisons

For three groups:

\[ \mu_1-\mu_2,\qquad \mu_1-\mu_3,\qquad \mu_2-\mu_3 \]

With \(k\) groups:

\[ m=\binom{k}{2} \]

Next step

Use pairwise confidence intervals to compare specific group means: first Fisher’s LSD, then Bonferroni.

8.5 Pairwise Confidence Intervals

General idea

After ANOVA rejects, compare two population means using an interval for \(\mu_i-\mu_j\):

\[ (\bar{x}_i-\bar{x}_j) \pm t^* \sqrt{ MSE\left(\frac{1}{n_i}+\frac{1}{n_j}\right) } \qquad \text{where } \hat{\sigma}^2=MSE \]

Fisher’s LSD

Use the ordinary two-sided critical value:

\[ t^*=t_{1-\alpha/2,\;n-k} \]

This gives narrower intervals, so it has more power to detect differences.

It is less conservative when many pairs are compared.

Bonferroni

First count the number of pairwise comparisons:

\[ m=\binom{k}{2} \]

Then use the adjusted critical value:

\[ t^*=t_{1-\alpha/(2m),\;n-k} \]

This gives wider intervals.

Decision rule

A pair is declared different when the interval for \(\mu_i-\mu_j\) does not contain 0.

If the interval contains 0, the pair is not clearly different.

So the whole method is:

\[ \text{interval avoids }0 \Rightarrow \text{different} \]

Fisher’s LSD: The Idea

Least Significant Difference

Fisher’s LSD compares two group means after the overall ANOVA test has rejected \(H_0\).

For a pair of groups \(i\) and \(j\), we compare the observed mean difference:

\[ \bar{x}_i-\bar{x}_j \]

Shared error estimate

Use the pooled within-group variation from ANOVA:

\[ MSE \]

This estimates the common variance \(\sigma^2\).

Ordinary t critical value

Fisher’s LSD uses:

\[ t^*=t_{1-\alpha/2,\;n-k} \]

The degrees of freedom come from the ANOVA error row.

LSD cutoff

When group sizes are \(n_i\) and \(n_j\):

\[ LSD= t^* \sqrt{ MSE\left(\frac{1}{n_i}+\frac{1}{n_j}\right) } \]

How to decide

If

\[ |\bar{x}_i-\bar{x}_j|>LSD, \]

then the pair is declared different. Fisher’s LSD is powerful, but it is less conservative when many pairwise comparisons are made.

Bonferroni: Why Adjust?

The issue

After ANOVA rejects, we may compare many pairs of means.

Each pairwise comparison has a chance of making a Type I error, so doing many comparisons increases the chance of making at least one false discovery.

1. Count comparisons

With \(k\) groups, the number of pairwise comparisons is

\[ m=\binom{k}{2}. \]

For example, if \(k=4\),

\[ m=\binom{4}{2}=6. \]

2. Experiment-wise error

As the number of pairwise comparisons increases, the chance of making at least one Type I error across the full set also increases.

If the comparisons were independent, this probability would be

\[ 1-(1-\alpha)^m. \]

Bonferroni does not require independence.

3. Bonferroni correction

To keep the overall error rate controlled, Bonferroni uses a smaller per-comparison level:

\[ \frac{\alpha}{m}. \]

For a two-sided interval, this gives

\[ t^*=t_{1-\alpha/(2m),\;n-k}. \]

Key idea

Protected Fisher’s LSD is used only after the overall ANOVA rejects. It gives narrower intervals and more power, but its protection against familywise Type I error weakens as the number of groups and pairwise comparisons increases.

Bonferroni is more cautious: it uses wider intervals to control the familywise Type I error rate across all planned pairwise comparisons.

Final Checklist

Before calculating ANOVA

  • Identify the response variable.
  • Identify the grouping variable.
  • State \(H_0:\mu_1=\cdots=\mu_k\).
  • Check independence, Normal errors, and equal variance.

After calculating ANOVA

  • Fill in df, SS, MS, and \(F^*\).
  • Use the right-tail F p-value.
  • Reject \(H_0\) when the p-value is less than or equal to \(\alpha\).
  • If ANOVA rejects, use pairwise comparisons.

One slide takeaway. ANOVA compares several means by asking whether between-group variation is large compared with within-group error variation.

Chapter Summary

Global ANOVA test Test

\[ H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2=\cdots=\mu_k \]

against the claim that not all population means are equal.

Variation and the F statistic ANOVA splits total variation into treatment and error variation, then compares them using

\[ F^*=\frac{MSTr}{MSE}. \]

After rejection A significant ANOVA result says at least one mean differs. Use Fisher’s LSD or Bonferroni intervals to determine which pairs differ.


One slide takeaway. Use ANOVA for the global question first, then use adjusted pairwise comparisons to investigate specific mean differences.
