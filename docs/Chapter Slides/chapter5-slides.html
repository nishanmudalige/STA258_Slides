5.1 Point Estimates and Interval Estimates

Point estimate

A single numerical value computed from sample data that serves as the best guess of an unknown population parameter.

Examples: \(\bar{x}\) estimates \(\mu\), \(s^2\) estimates \(\sigma^2\), and \(\hat p\) estimates \(p\).

Confidence interval

A plausible range of values for a population parameter, built from sample data and a specified confidence level.

Big idea. A statistic will usually not equal the parameter exactly. The interval tells us how far away the statistic could reasonably be.

The CI Skeleton

All confidence intervals in this chapter are variations of:

\[ \text{estimate} \pm \underbrace{(\text{critical value})(\text{standard error})}_{\text{margin of error}} \]

Estimate \(\bar{x}\), \(\hat p\), \(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2\), \(\hat p_1-\hat p_2\), \(s^2\), or \(s_1^2/s_2^2\).

Critical value From \(Z\), \(t\), \(\chi^2\), or \(F\), depending on the sampling distribution.

Standard error The standard deviation of the estimator, or an estimate of it.

5.2 Interpreting Confidence

For a \(C\%\) confidence interval:

In repeated sampling, about \(C\%\) of intervals constructed using this method will capture the true parameter.

Do not say: “There is a 95% probability that this particular interval contains \(\mu\).”

The parameter is fixed. The random object is the interval produced by the sample.

CI Reference Guide

Target parameter Situation Reference distribution Interval shape
\(\mu\) one mean, \(\sigma\) known \(Z\) \(\bar{x}\pm z^*\sigma/\sqrt n\)
\(\mu\) one mean, \(\sigma\) unknown \(t_{n-1}\) \(\bar{x}\pm t^*s/\sqrt n\)
\(p\) one proportion \(Z\) \(\hat p\pm z^*\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\)
\(\sigma^2\) one variance, normal population \(\chi^2_{n-1}\) asymmetric
\(\mu_1-\mu_2\) two independent means \(Z\) or \(t\) difference \(\pm\) margin
\(p_1-p_2\) two independent proportions \(Z\) difference \(\pm\) margin
\(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_2^2\) ratio of variances \(F\) asymmetric
\(\mu_d\) paired data \(t_{n-1}\) \(\bar d\pm t^*s_d/\sqrt n\)

5.3.1.1 One Mean, Known \(\sigma\)

When the population standard deviation \(\sigma\) is known:

\[ \bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \]

Use this for a population mean \(\mu\) with known \(\sigma\).

Assumptions

  • Random sample or randomized experiment.
  • Independent observations.
  • Population is normal, or \(n\) is large enough for the CLT.

Exam cue If the problem gives a population standard deviation, use \(z\). If it gives a sample standard deviation, use \(t\).

Example: Known Sigma - Magazine Readers

Playbill magazine reports \(\bar{x}=119{,}155\) dollars from \(n=80\) households. Assume \(\sigma=30{,}000\) dollars.

Construct 90%, 95%, and 99% confidence intervals for the population mean household income of readers.

Level Critical value Margin of error Confidence interval
90% 1.645 \(1.645(30000/\sqrt{80})=5517.38\) \((113637.62, 124672.38)\)
95% 1.960 \(1.960(30000/\sqrt{80})=6573.04\) \((112581.96, 125728.04)\)
99% 2.576 \(2.576(30000/\sqrt{80})=8640.41\) \((110514.59, 127795.41)\)

We are 95% confident that the mean household income of Playbill readers is between about 112,582 dollars and 125,728 dollars.

Example: Known Sigma - Car Sales

Annual car sales are normally distributed with known \(\sigma=15\). For a random sample of 15 salespeople:

\[79,43,58,66,101,63,79,33,58,71,60,101,74,55,88\]

Construct a 95% CI for \(\mu\).

\(\bar{x}=68.6\), so the interval is approximately \((61.01, 76.19)\). We are 95% confident that the population mean number of cars sold annually is between about 61 and 76.

5.3.1.2 One Mean, Unknown \(\sigma\)

When \(\sigma\) is unknown, estimate it with \(s\) and use the Student \(t\) distribution:

\[ \bar{x} \pm t_{n-1,\alpha/2}\frac{s}{\sqrt n} \]

Why t? Replacing \(\sigma\) with \(s\) adds uncertainty. The \(t\) distribution has heavier tails, especially for small degrees of freedom.

Small samples For small \(n\), check that the population is reasonably normal or that the sample has no severe skew/outliers.

Example: Unknown Sigma - Ancient Air

Nitrogen percentages from air bubbles trapped in ancient amber:

\[63.4,65.0,64.4,63.3,54.8,64.5,60.8,49.1,51.0\]

Construct a 90% CI for the mean nitrogen percentage.

Using \(n=9\), \(\bar{x}=59.5889\), \(s=6.2553\), and \(t^*=1.8595\):

\[59.5889\pm1.8595\frac{6.2553}{3}=(55.71,63.47).\]

Example: Unknown Sigma - Camera Photos

A random sample of 10 digital camera owners has stored photo counts:

\[25,6,22,26,31,18,13,20,14,2\]

Construct a 95% CI for the population mean number of stored photos.

\(\bar{x}=17.7\), \(s=9.0805\), \(df=9\), and \(t^*=2.262\). The 95% CI is approximately \((11.20,24.20)\) photos.

5.3.2 One Proportion

For a population proportion \(p\), with \(\hat p=x/n\):

\[ \hat p \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}} \]

Conditions

  • Random sample.
  • Independent Bernoulli trials.
  • Large counts: \(n\hat p \ge 10\) and \(n(1-\hat p)\ge 10\).

Boundary check The final interval should make sense for a proportion. Values below 0 or above 1 need careful handling.

Example: One Proportion - Transit Use

A university survey asks 1200 students whether they use public transportation. Among them, 738 say yes.

Construct a 95% CI for the true proportion of students who use public transportation.

\[ \hat p=\frac{738}{1200}=0.6150 \]

\[ SE=\sqrt{\frac{0.6150(0.3850)}{1200}}=0.01405 \]

\[ 0.6150\pm1.96(0.01405)=(0.5875,0.6425) \]

We are 95% confident that between 58.75% and 64.25% of students use public transportation.

Example: One Proportion - Work From Home

In a survey of 650 employees, 286 prefer working from home at least three days per week.

Construct a 90% CI for the population proportion.

\(\hat p=0.44\), so the 90% CI is approximately \((0.4079,0.4721)\).

  • \(n=50\), \(\hat p=0.10\)
  • \(n=100\), \(\hat p=0.20\)
  • \(n=15\), \(\hat p=0.50\)

5.3.3 One Variance

For a normally distributed population, a confidence interval for \(\sigma^2\) is:

\[ \left( \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{\alpha/2,n-1}}, \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{1-\alpha/2,n-1}} \right) \]

This interval is asymmetric. Normality matters here much more than it does for large-sample mean intervals.

Example: One Variance - Bottle Filling

A random sample of 25 bottles has sample variance \(s^2=12.5\). Assume filling amounts are normally distributed.

Construct a 95% CI for \(\sigma^2\).

\[df=24,\quad \chi^2_{0.975,24}=39.364,\quad \chi^2_{0.025,24}=12.401\]

\[ \text{Lower}=\frac{24(12.5)}{39.364}=7.62,\qquad \text{Upper}=\frac{24(12.5)}{12.401}=24.19 \]

The 95% CI for the population variance is approximately \((7.62,24.19)\).

Example: One Variance - Reaction Times

A random sample of 16 drivers has sample variance \(s^2=0.36\). Assume reaction times are normally distributed.

Construct a 90% CI for \(\sigma^2\).

The 90% CI is approximately \((0.2160,0.7437)\).

5.4 Two-Sample Confidence Intervals

A two-sample confidence interval estimates a population quantity that compares two groups.

The groups must be clearly labelled, because the order matters:

\[ \text{Group 1} - \text{Group 2} \]

Changing the order changes the sign of the interval.

Difference of means

\[ \mu_1-\mu_2 \]

Used for comparing two population averages.

Difference of proportions

\[ p_1-p_2 \]

Used for comparing two population percentages.

Ratio of variances

\[ \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_2^2} \]

Used for comparing two population variances.

5.4.1 Confidence Interval for a Difference of Means

For two independent populations, we often want to estimate:

\[ \mu_1-\mu_2 \]

A natural statistic is:

\[ \bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2 \]

Case 1 \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\) known

Use a \(z\) interval.

Rare in practice.

Case 2 \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\) unknown, but assumed equal

Use a pooled two-sample \(t\) interval.

Case 3 \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\) unknown and not assumed equal

Use Welch’s two-sample \(t\) interval.

Difference of Means: Known \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\)

When the population standard deviations are known, a level \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\) is:

\[ (\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2) \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \sqrt{ \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2} } \]

This case is mathematically useful but rare in real applications, because population standard deviations are usually unknown.

Example 1: Known \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\)

Suppose final marks in the same course are compared between two campuses.

UTSG: \(n_1=64\), \(\bar{x}_1=76\), known \(\sigma_1=10\).
UTM: \(n_2=81\), \(\bar{x}_2=72\), known \(\sigma_2=12\).

Construct a 95% CI for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\).

\[ \bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2=4 \]

\[ SE=\sqrt{\frac{10^2}{64}+\frac{12^2}{81}}=1.827 \]

\[ 4\pm1.96(1.827)=(0.42,7.58) \]

We are 95% confident that the UTSG population mean is between 0.42 and 7.58 marks higher than the UTM population mean.

Difference of Means: Equal Unknown Variances

If \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\) are unknown but assumed equal, we pool the two sample variances.

The pooled variance is:

\[ s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2} \]

The level \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\) is:

\[ (\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2) \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\;n_1+n_2-2} s_p \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}} \]

Use this only when the equal-variance assumption is reasonable. Otherwise, use Welch’s interval.

Example 1: Pooled Two-Sample \(t\) Interval

A researcher compares battery life for two brands of the same device.

Brand A: \(n_1=12\), \(\bar{x}_1=52.4\), \(s_1=6.1\).
Brand B: \(n_2=10\), \(\bar{x}_2=47.8\), \(s_2=5.4\).

Assume equal population variances. Construct a 95% CI for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\).

\[ s_p^2=\frac{11(6.1^2)+9(5.4^2)}{20}=33.493,\quad s_p=5.787 \]

\[ SE=5.787\sqrt{\frac{1}{12}+\frac{1}{10}}=2.476,\quad t^*_{20}=2.086 \]

\[ 4.6\pm2.086(2.476)=(-0.57,9.77) \]

Because the interval contains 0, the data do not clearly show a nonzero mean battery-life difference.

Example 2: Pooled Two-Sample \(t\) Interval

Method A: \(n_1=15\), \(\bar{x}_1=84.2\), \(s_1=7.5\).
Method B: \(n_2=14\), \(\bar{x}_2=78.6\), \(s_2=6.8\).

Assume equal variances. Construct a 90% CI for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\).

The 90% CI is approximately \((1.06,10.14)\) points. Method A appears to have a higher mean quiz score.

Difference of Means: Unequal Unknown Variances

If the population variances are unknown and are not assumed equal, use Welch’s two-sample \(t\) interval:

\[ (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,df} \sqrt{ \frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2} } \]

This estimates:

\[ \mu_1 - \mu_2 \]

when the two samples are independent and the equal-variance assumption is not reasonable.

Course note. Welch’s method is the more flexible default when the two sample variances look meaningfully different.

Welch Degrees of Freedom

Degrees of freedom note

In this course, we are not testing your ability to be a calculator in an exam setting.

For hand calculations, you may use the conservative degrees of freedom:

\[ df = \min(n_1 - 1,\; n_2 - 1) \]

A more accurate approximation, commonly used by software, is the Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom:

\[ df = \frac{ \left(\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2 }{ \frac{\left(s_1^2/n_1\right)^2}{n_1-1} + \frac{\left(s_2^2/n_2\right)^2}{n_2-1} } \]

For exams in this course, use the conservative version unless told otherwise.

Example 1: Welch Two-Sample \(t\) Interval

Group 1: \(n_1=25\), \(\bar{x}_1=42\), \(s_1=14\).
Group 2: \(n_2=30\), \(\bar{x}_2=35\), \(s_2=9\).

Assume unequal variances. Construct a 95% CI for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\).

\[ SE=\sqrt{\frac{14^2}{25}+\frac{9^2}{30}}=3.245 \]

Using Welch \(df\approx38.6\), \(t^*\approx2.023\):

\[ 7\pm2.023(3.245)=(0.43,13.57) \]

The interval is entirely positive, suggesting Group 1 has a higher mean commute time.

Example 2: Welch Two-Sample \(t\) Interval

Group 1: \(n_1=18\), \(\bar{x}_1=96\), \(s_1=22\).
Group 2: \(n_2=22\), \(\bar{x}_2=82\), \(s_2=16\).

Assume unequal variances. Construct a 90% CI for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\).

The 90% CI is approximately \((3.47,24.53)\). Group 1 appears to have a higher mean weekly food spending.

Assumptions for Two-Sample Mean Intervals

Independent samples

The two samples must be independent. One person’s measurement in group 1 should not determine a person’s measurement in group 2.

Normality or large samples

If sample sizes are small, each population should be approximately normal. For larger samples, the CLT makes the interval more reliable.

Randomization

Each group should come from a random sample or a randomized experiment.

Equal variances only for pooled intervals

The pooled method requires the extra assumption that \(\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2\).

5.4.2 Confidence Interval for a Difference of Proportions

For two independent samples, let:

\[ \hat{p}_1=\frac{x_1}{n_1}, \qquad \hat{p}_2=\frac{x_2}{n_2} \]

A level \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(p_1-p_2\) is:

\[ (\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2) \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \sqrt{ \frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2} } \]

The interval estimates the difference between two population proportions, not the difference between two sample counts.

Example 1: Difference of Proportions - Left-Handed Students

Campus 1 has 18 left-handed students out of 120. Campus 2 has 10 left-handed students out of 100.

Construct a 95% CI for \(p_1-p_2\).

\[ \hat p_1=0.1500,\quad \hat p_2=0.1000,\quad \hat p_1-\hat p_2=0.0500 \]

\[ SE=\sqrt{\frac{0.15(0.85)}{120}+\frac{0.10(0.90)}{100}}=0.0443 \]

\[ 0.05\pm1.96(0.0443)=(-0.0368,0.1368) \]

The interval includes 0, so a zero difference in population proportions is plausible.

Example 2: Difference of Proportions - Policy Support

City 1 has 210 supporters out of 500. City 2 has 170 supporters out of 450.

Construct a 90% CI for \(p_1-p_2\).

The 90% CI is approximately \((-0.010,0.095)\). Because 0 is inside, the data do not clearly establish a higher support rate for City 1.

Assumptions for Difference of Proportions

Randomization

Each sample should be random, or the data should come from a randomized experiment.

Independent groups

The two groups being compared must be independent from each other.

10% condition

If sampling without replacement, each sample should be less than 10% of its population.

Success-failure condition

Each group should have enough successes and failures:

\[ x_i \ge 10, \qquad n_i-x_i \ge 10 \]

  • The two population variances must be equal
  • Each group should have enough successes and failures
  • The sample sizes must be exactly the same

5.4.3 Confidence Interval for a Ratio of Variances

To compare two population variances, the parameter is:

\[ \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_2^2} \]

If both populations are normal and the samples are independent, then:

\[ \frac{s_1^2/\sigma_1^2}{s_2^2/\sigma_2^2} \sim F_{n_1-1,\;n_2-1} \]

A ratio near 1 suggests similar variances. A ratio far above or below 1 suggests one population is more variable.

Ratio of Variances: Formula

A level \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_2^2\) is:

\[ \left( \frac{s_1^2/s_2^2}{F_{1-\alpha/2,\;n_1-1,\;n_2-1}}, \; \frac{s_1^2/s_2^2}{F_{\alpha/2,\;n_1-1,\;n_2-1}} \right) \]

This interval is sensitive to the normality assumption. For variance ratios, normality matters more than it does for large-sample mean intervals.

Example 1: Ratio of Variances - Machines

Machine 1: \(n_1=16\), \(s_1=12\). Machine 2: \(n_2=14\), \(s_2=8\).

Assuming normal populations, construct a 95% CI for \(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_2^2\).

The 95% CI is approximately \((0.74,6.58)\). Since 1 is inside, equal variances are plausible.

Example 2: Ratio of Variances - Labs

Lab 1: \(n_1=21\), \(s_1=5.5\). Lab 2: \(n_2=19\), \(s_2=3.8\).

Construct a 90% CI for \(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_2^2\).

The 90% CI is approximately \((0.96,4.51)\). Since 1 is inside, the evidence for different variances is not decisive.

Assumptions for Ratio of Variances

Independent samples

The two samples must be independent from each other.

Random sampling

Each sample should be obtained through a random process.

Normal populations

The two populations should be approximately normal. This is especially important for inference about variances.

Same measurement scale

The two variables should be measured on the same or comparable scale.

Question

  • The two sample means must be equal
  • Equal population variances are plausible
  • The two sample sizes must be equal
  • The first variance must be larger than the second variance

5.5 Confidence Intervals on Paired Data

Paired data occur when observations are naturally matched.

Instead of comparing two independent sample means, we compute differences:

\[ d_i = x_{2i} - x_{1i} \]

Then we build a one-sample \(t\) interval for the population mean difference \(\mu_d\).

Common paired settings

  • Before and after measurements on the same person
  • Matched twins
  • Same student under two testing conditions
  • Same object measured by two devices

Do not treat paired data as two independent samples. The pairing is the main information.

Paired Data: Formula

Let \(d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_n\) be the paired differences.

The level \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu_d\) is:

\[ \bar{d} \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\;n-1} \frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}} \]

where \(\bar{d}\) is the sample mean of the differences and \(s_d\) is the sample standard deviation of the differences.

The normality condition applies to the differences, not necessarily to the original two measurements separately.

Visual: Paired Data Means Differences

Each line represents one student. The paired analysis uses the within student change, not two unrelated groups.

Example 1: Paired Data - Tutoring Scores

Ten students take a quiz before and after tutoring. Differences are after minus before:

\[5,2,5,2,4,4,4,4,3,5\]

Construct a 95% CI for the mean improvement.

The 95% CI is approximately \((2.99,4.61)\) points. We are 95% confident that the mean improvement is positive.

Example 2: Paired Data - Blood Pressure

Twelve patients have blood pressure measured before and after treatment. Let \(d=\) before minus after:

\[6,8,3,10,7,5,9,4,6,8,7,5\]

Construct a 90% CI for the mean reduction.

The 90% CI is approximately \((5.43,7.57)\). Positive values mean the treatment lowers blood pressure on average.

Assumptions for Paired Data

Paired observations

Each pair must come from the same unit or from meaningfully matched units.

Independent pairs

The pairs themselves should be independent of each other.

Normal differences

The distribution of differences should be approximately normal, especially for small samples.

Random sampling

The sample of pairs should come from a random process or a randomized design.

A group of students takes a test before and after a review session. Which quantity should be used to build the confidence interval?

  • The mean of the before scores only
  • The mean of the after scores only
  • The mean of the paired differences
  • The ratio of the two sample variances

5.6 Sample Size Selection Using Confidence Intervals

Sample size selection works backwards.

Instead of asking:

What margin of error do we get from this sample size?

we ask:

What sample size do we need to achieve this margin of error?

For a mean

The required sample size depends on:

  • confidence level
  • desired margin of error
  • population standard deviation, or an estimate of it

For a proportion

The required sample size depends on:

  • confidence level
  • desired margin of error
  • planning value \(p^*\)

5.6.1 Sample Size for a Mean When \(\sigma\) is Known

For a confidence interval on a population mean with known \(\sigma\):

\[ E = z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]

Solving for \(n\) gives:

\[ n = \left( \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}\sigma}{E} \right)^2 \]

Always round up to the next whole number. Rounding down would make the margin of error too large.

Example 1: Sample Size for a Mean

A lab measurement has known \(\sigma=0.0068\) grams per liter. Management wants results accurate within \(E=0.005\) with 95% confidence.

How many repeated measurements are needed?

\[ n=\left(\frac{1.96(0.0068)}{0.005}\right)^2=7.10 \]

Round up: the lab needs \(n=8\) measurements.

Example 2: Sample Size for a Mean

A planning value is \(\sigma=22.50\) and the desired margin is \(E=2\).

Find the sample size for 90%, 95%, and 99% confidence.

Level Critical value Calculation Required \(n\)
90% 1.645 \((1.645(22.5)/2)^2=342.41\) 343
95% 1.960 \((1.960(22.5)/2)^2=486.20\) 487
99% 2.576 \((2.576(22.5)/2)^2=839.45\) 840

Higher confidence requires a larger sample size when the desired margin of error stays fixed.

Sample Size for a Mean When \(\sigma\) is Unknown

When \(\sigma\) is unknown, we use a preliminary estimate \(s\) from a pilot study or previous data.

A practical formula is:

\[ n = \left( \frac{t_{1-\alpha/2,\;n-1}s}{E} \right)^2 \]

but the \(t\) critical value depends on \(n\), the quantity we are trying to find.

This is why an iterative approach is used:

  1. Start with the \(z\) approximation.
  2. Use that \(n\) to get a \(t\) critical value.
  3. Recalculate \(n\).
  4. Repeat until \(n\) stabilizes.
  5. Round up.

Example 1: Sample Size, Sigma Unknown

A pilot study estimates the standard deviation of response time as \(s=12\) seconds. A researcher wants a 95% CI with margin of error at most \(E=5\) seconds.

Find the required sample size using the iterative \(t\) approach.

The sample size stabilizes at \(n=25\), so the researcher needs at least 25 observations.

Example 2: Sample Size, Sigma Unknown

A pilot study estimates the standard deviation of monthly textbook spending as \(s=18\). A 90% CI is desired with margin of error at most \(E=4\).

Find the required sample size using the iterative \(t\) approach.

The sample size stabilizes at \(n=57\).

5.6.2 Sample Size for a Proportion

For a confidence interval on a population proportion, the margin of error is approximately:

\[ E = z_{1-\alpha/2} \sqrt{ \frac{p^*(1-p^*)}{n} } \]

Solving for \(n\) gives:

\[ n = \left( \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{E} \right)^2 p^*(1-p^*) \]

The value \(p^*\) is a planning value. If no prior information is available, use \(p^*=0.5\) because it gives the most conservative sample size.

Example 1: Sample Size for a Proportion

A mayoral survey needs to estimate a candidate’s support with 95% confidence and margin of error no greater than 0.03. No prior estimate is available.

\[ n=\left(\frac{1.96}{0.03}\right)^2(0.5)(0.5)=1067.11 \]

Round up: contact at least \(n=1068\) voters.

Example 2: Sample Size for a Proportion

In 2007, 15.6% of people in the USA were not covered by health care insurance. Use \(p^*=0.156\) and \(E=0.03\).

Find the required sample size for 95% and 99% confidence.

\[ n_{95}=\left(\frac{1.96}{0.03}\right)^2(0.156)(0.844)=562.54\Rightarrow563 \]

\[ n_{99}=\left(\frac{2.576}{0.03}\right)^2(0.156)(0.844)=970.97\Rightarrow971 \]

The 99% confidence level requires a larger survey because the critical value is larger.

Question

When planning a confidence interval for a population proportion, no prior estimate of \(p\) is available. What value should we use for \(p^*\)?

  • \(p^*=0\)
  • \(p^*=0.25\)
  • \(p^*=0.5\)
  • \(p^*=1\)

Final Checklist

Before calculating - Identify the parameter. - Check one sample vs two sample vs paired. - Check whether the target is a mean, proportion, or variance. - Pick the reference distribution.

After calculating - Include units when relevant. - Interpret in context. - Check whether 0 or 1 has a special meaning. - For sample size, round up.

One slide takeaway. Confidence intervals are not separate tricks. They are the same idea with different estimators, standard errors, and reference distributions.
