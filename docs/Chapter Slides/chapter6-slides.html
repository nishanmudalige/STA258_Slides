6.1 What a Hypothesis Test Does

High Level Idea A hypothesis test uses sample evidence to decide whether there is enough evidence against a claim about a population parameter.

Null hypothesis, \(H_0\) The status quo, no-effect, or no-difference claim.

Alternative hypothesis, \(H_a\) The direction of evidence we are trying to detect.

The test is performed assuming \(H_0\) is true. The p-value measures how unusual the data are under that assumption.

The Five-Step Procedure

  1. 1. Choose \(\alpha\). Set the significance level before making the decision.
  2. 2. State hypotheses. Use the correct population parameter.
  3. 3. Compute statistic. Standardize the estimate with the correct standard error.
  4. 4. Find the p-value. Use the tail area that matches \(H_a\).
  5. 5. Conclude. Compare to \(\alpha\) and write the conclusion in context.

Step 4 and Step 5 are separate: first calculate evidence, then make the decision.

Choosing the Alternative

Left-tailed \[H_a:\theta < \theta_0\] Use when the research question asks whether the parameter decreased or is smaller.

Right-tailed \[H_a:\theta > \theta_0\] Use when the research question asks whether the parameter increased or is larger.

Two-sided \[H_a:\theta \ne \theta_0\] Use when the research question asks whether the parameter changed in either direction.

P-Value Areas

  1. Left-tailed: \(P(W \le w^*)\).
  2. Right-tailed: \(P(W \ge w^*)\).
  3. Two-tailed: double the matching one-tail area.

\(W\) is the test statistic from the chosen reference distribution (Z, t, chi-square, or F), with observed value \(w^*\).

Use the two dropdowns at the top-right of the plot: first pick the type of test (\(Z\), \(t\), \(\chi^2\), or \(F\)), then pick the alternative (left-tailed, right-tailed, or two-tailed). The shaded p-value area updates for that combination.

Decision Rule

If p-value \(\le \alpha\) Reject \(H_0\) in favor of \(H_a\).

If p-value \(> \alpha\) Fail to reject \(H_0\).

Careful language Failing to reject \(H_0\) does not prove \(H_0\). It only means the sample does not provide enough evidence against it at the chosen significance level.

Reading Strength of Evidence

p-value range Textbook language
\(\le 0.001\) Very strong evidence against \(H_0\)
\(0.001\) to \(0.01\) Strong evidence
\(0.01\) to \(0.05\) Evidence
\(0.05\) to \(0.075\) Some evidence
\(0.075\) to \(0.10\) Slight evidence
\(>0.10\) Little or no evidence against \(H_0\)

Statistical significance does not measure practical importance. Pair the p-value with an estimate or confidence interval.

Question

A test gives p-value \(0.032\) and uses \(\alpha=0.05\). What should we do?

  • Fail to reject \(H_0\)
  • Reject \(H_0\)
  • Prove \(H_0\) is false
  • Increase \(\alpha\) until the test works

6.2.1.1 Hypothesis Tests on the Mean, \(\sigma\) Known

For testing \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\) when \(\sigma\) is known, use the test statistic

\[ z^*=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n} \]

which, under \(H_0\), follows the standard normal reference distribution \(Z\sim N(0,1)\).

Reference distribution \(Z^*\sim N(0,1)\).

Use when Testing a population mean with known \(\sigma\) and an appropriate random/normal-or-large-sample setup.

Example: Coffee Fill Volume

The cafe chain Second Cup advertises that a large iced latte is filled to a target of \(\mu_0=7.2\) oz of base. Customers complain the cups feel light, so quality control pulls a random sample of \(n=40\) cups.

From years of calibration records, the filling machine’s standard deviation is known: \(\sigma=1.1\) oz. The sample mean is \(\bar{x}=6.7\) oz.

Test whether the true mean fill volume has dropped below 7.2 oz at \(\alpha=0.05\).

What we are given

  • Target mean: \(\mu_0 = 7.2\) oz
  • Known SD: \(\sigma = 1.1\) oz
  • Sample: \(n = 40\), \(\bar{x} = 6.7\) oz
  • Significance level: \(\alpha = 0.05\)

Which test? \(\sigma\) is known, so this is a one-sample \(z\) test with reference distribution \(N(0,1)\).

Coffee Example: Calculation

Hypotheses \[H_0:\mu=7.2 \qquad H_a:\mu<7.2 \quad (\alpha=0.05)\]

Test statistic \[z^*=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}=\frac{6.7-7.2}{1.1/\sqrt{40}}=-2.8748\]

p-value \[\text{p-value}=P(Z\le -2.8748)=0.00202\]

Decision \(0.00202 \le 0.05\), so reject \(H_0\).

Conclusion There is sufficient evidence that the mean fill volume is below 7.2 oz.

6.2.1.2 Hypothesis Tests on the Mean, \(\sigma\) Unknown

For testing \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\) when \(\sigma\) is unknown, replace it with \(s\) and use the test statistic

\[ t^*=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt n} \]

which, under \(H_0\), follows the Student \(t\) distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.

Reference distribution \(t^*\sim t_{n-1}\).

Assumptions Random/independent observations; population normal or sample large enough for the \(t\) approximation.

Example: Food Delivery ETA

The delivery app Uber Eats promises an average drop-off time of \(16.5\) minutes. Suspecting orders really take longer, a student logs the wait (in minutes) on 12 random orders:

16.2, 18.4, 15.7, 19.1, 17.3, 16.9, 20.4, 18.2, 15.9, 17.6, 16.7, 19.8

Test whether the true mean delivery time is greater than the advertised 16.5 minutes.

\[H_0:\mu=16.5,\qquad H_a:\mu>16.5\] \(\sigma\) is unknown, so use a one-sample \(t\) test.

Check in R: Food Delivery ETA

The output gives \(t=2.682\), \(df=11\), and p-value \(0.0107\). At \(\alpha=0.05\), reject \(H_0\).

6.2.2 Hypothesis Tests on a Proportion

For testing \(H_0:p=p_0\), use the test statistic

\[ z^*=\frac{\hat p-p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}} \]

which, under \(H_0\), follows the standard normal reference distribution \(Z\sim N(0,1)\).

Reference distribution \(z^*\sim N(0,1)\).

Use the null value The standard error uses \(p_0\), not \(\hat p\).

Large-count check Under \(H_0\), both \(np_0\) and \(n(1-p_0)\) should be at least 10.

Example: Reusable Cup Discount

Last year only \(10\%\) of students used the reusable-cup discount at a campus cafe. After a sustainability campaign, a fresh random survey finds \(x=68\) of \(n=520\) students bringing their own cup.

Is this evidence that the participation rate has increased above \(10\%\)?

\[H_0:p=0.10,\qquad H_a:p>0.10\]

\[\hat p=\frac{68}{520}=0.1308\]

Check in R: One Proportion

Question

For the recycling test, why is the alternative \(H_a:p>0.10\)?

  • The sample proportion is always greater than the null value
  • The research question asks whether participation has increased
  • The sample size is greater than 30
  • Proportion tests are always right-tailed

6.2.3 One Variance Test

For one normal population, test a variance using the chi-square statistic:

\[ \chi^{2*}=\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma_0^2} \]

Reference distribution \(\chi^2\) with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.

Important condition The population should be approximately normal. Variance tests are sensitive to non-normality.

Example: Protein Scoop Consistency

A fitness brand claims its protein powder is dosed so precisely that scoop weights have standard deviation at most \(2.8\) g. A reviewer weighs a random sample of \(n=28\) scoops and finds \(s=3.4\) g.

Does this undermine the consistency claim? Use \(\alpha=0.05\) and assume scoop weights are normal.

\[H_0:\sigma^2=2.8^2,\qquad H_a:\sigma^2>2.8^2\]

A larger sample spread is evidence against the “at most 2.8 g” claim.

Check in R: Scoop Variance Test

6.3 Two-Sample Test Targets

Target parameter Typical question Reference distribution
\(\mu_1-\mu_2\) Are two population means different? \(Z\) or \(t\)
\(p_1-p_2\) Are two population proportions different? \(Z\)
\(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_2^2\) Are two population variances different? \(F\)
\(\mu_d\) Is the mean paired difference nonzero? \(t\)

Start by deciding whether the samples are independent or paired. That choice changes the whole test.

6.3.1.1 Two Means: Known \(\sigma_1,\sigma_2\)

When both population standard deviations are known:

\[ z^*= \frac{(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2)-D_0} {\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1+\sigma_2^2/n_2}} \]

Usually \(D_0=0\) when testing whether the population means are equal.

Use \(Z\) because the population standard deviations are known.

6.3.1.2 Two Means: Unknown Variances

For unequal or unspecified population variances, use Welch’s two-sample \(t\) test:

\[ t^*= \frac{(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2)-D_0} {\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}} \]

Default Welch is a good default when equal variances are not clearly justified.

Degrees of freedom Software uses the Welch-Satterthwaite approximation.

Example: Active Recall vs Rereading

Decades of memory research suggest active recall (self-testing) beats passively rereading notes. Two independent tutorial sections each prepare with one method, then write the same quiz.

Active recall: 88 91 85 94 89 90 87 92 86 93 89 90

Rereading: 84 86 82 88 85 83 81 87 84 86

Does active recall produce a higher mean quiz score?

Let \(\mu_1\) be the active-recall mean and \(\mu_2\) the rereading mean. Test \(H_0:\mu_1-\mu_2=0\) against \(H_a:\mu_1-\mu_2>0\).

Check in R: Two-Sample t Test

6.3.2 Difference of Proportions

When testing \(H_0:p_1-p_2=0\), pool the sample proportions under the null:

\[ \hat p=\frac{x_1+x_2}{n_1+n_2} \]

\[ z^*=\frac{\hat p_1-\hat p_2} {\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)(1/n_1+1/n_2)}} \]

Example: Push Notifications

A habit-tracking app runs an experiment: half of new users get a daily push notification, half get none. For each user the app records whether they completed a workout that day.

Push notification: \(142\) check-ins out of \(360\) users

No notification: \(118\) check-ins out of \(370\) users

Test whether the daily push notification increases the check-in rate.

\[ H_0:p_1-p_2=0,\qquad H_a:p_1-p_2>0 \]

Check in R: Two Proportions

The output gives p-value about \(0.0166\), so there is evidence that reminder texts increase the sign-up rate.

6.3.3 Ratio of Variances

For two normal populations, test a ratio of variances with

\[ F^*=\frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2} \]

using an \(F\) reference distribution with \(n_1-1\) and \(n_2-1\) degrees of freedom.

Normality matters The \(F\) test for variances is sensitive to non-normality.

Two-sided idea Extreme ratios in either direction count as evidence against equal variances.

Example: Scoring Consistency

Two basketball players average about the same points per game, but a scout suspects Player A is less consistent — more variable game to game.

Player A: 78 85 72 91 68 88 75 82 95 70 84 77

Player B: 82 84 79 85 81 83 80 86 82 84

Test whether Player A has the larger scoring variance at \(\alpha=0.05\).

\[H_0:\sigma_A^2=\sigma_B^2,\qquad H_a:\sigma_A^2>\sigma_B^2\]

\[F^*=\frac{s_A^2}{s_B^2}\sim F_{n_A-1,\,n_B-1}\]

Check in R: Ratio of Variances

var.test reports \(F^*\), the two degrees of freedom, and the p-value. An \(F^*\) well above 1 is evidence that Player A’s scoring is more variable.

6.4 Paired Data

For matched pairs or before-after data, first reduce the data to differences:

\[ d_i = \text{new}_i-\text{current}_i \]

Then run a one-sample \(t\) test on \(\mu_d\).

Not independent groups The two measurements inside a pair are linked.

Reference distribution \(t\) with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom for the differences.

Example: Pomodoro Focus Timer

Each of 12 students completes a timed practice set, then writes a comparable set after a week of studying with the Pomodoro technique (25-minute focus blocks). Scores are paired within each student.

Before: 34 29 41 38 36 33 40 31 37 35 39 32

After: 36 31 43 39 38 35 39 34 40 37 41 33

Can we conclude the technique improves scores?

Use paired differences \(d_i=\text{after}_i-\text{before}_i\) and test \(H_0:\mu_d=0\) against \(H_a:\mu_d>0\).

Question

In the Pomodoro example, why is a paired \(t\) test appropriate?

  • The two samples have the same mean
  • The sample size is less than 30
  • Each student has both before and after scores, so observations are matched within student
  • Proportion tests cannot use score data

Assumptions: Two Independent Means

Sampling structure
  • The two samples are independent.
  • Each sample is random, or the data come from a randomized design.
  • If sampling without replacement, each sample is less than 10% of its population.
Model condition
  • Each population is approximately normal, or both sample sizes are large enough for the CLT.
  • For a pooled test, equal population variances should be reasonable.

Assumptions: Difference of Proportions

Sampling structure
  • The two groups are independent.
  • Each sample is random, or the data come from a randomized design.
  • If sampling without replacement, each sample should be less than 10% of its population.
Large-count condition
  • Check successes and failures in each group.
  • At least 10 successes: \(x_i \ge 10\).
  • At least 10 failures: \(n_i - x_i \ge 10\).

Assumptions: Ratio of Variances

Required structure
  • The two samples are independent.
  • Each sample comes from a random process or randomized design.
Normality matters here
  • The \(F\) test for a variance ratio is sensitive to non-normality.
  • This condition is more important here than it is for many mean-based tests.

Assumptions: Paired Data

Paired structure
  • Each pair comes from the same unit, matched units, or a before-after measurement.
  • The pairs themselves are independent of one another.
Model condition
  • Analyze the differences \(d_i\), not the two original columns separately.
  • For small samples, the distribution of differences should be approximately normal.

Choosing the Right Test

If the problem asks for… Use…
one mean and gives \(\sigma\) one-sample \(z\) test
one mean and gives \(s\) or raw data one-sample \(t\) test
one proportion one-proportion \(z\) test
one variance chi-square test
difference of means, known \(\sigma_1,\sigma_2\) two-sample \(z\) test
difference of means, equal variances pooled two-sample \(t\) test
difference of means, unequal/unspecified variances Welch two-sample \(t\) test
difference of proportions two-proportion \(z\) test
ratio of variances \(F\) test
before-after or matched data paired \(t\) test on differences

What to Write in a Conclusion

Decision Reject or fail to reject \(H_0\).

Evidence strength Use the p-value to describe how strong the evidence is.

Context Name the population parameter and the direction of the claim.

Good conclusion: “There is sufficient evidence at the 5% level that the mean fill volume for large coffees is below 7.2 oz.”
