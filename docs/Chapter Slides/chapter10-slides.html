Why Categorical Data Need a New Tool

So far, many methods focused on means, variances, or proportions.

In this chapter, the data are often counts across categories.

Examples:

  • Which app did a student choose?
  • Which colour did a customer prefer?
  • Which pickup time did an order fall into?
  • Which outcome happened in a repeated experiment?

The question is no longer “What is the average?”
The question is usually: Do the observed counts match what the model predicted?

From Binomial to Chi-square

For two categories, one count can be modelled with a binomial distribution.

If \(Y_1 \sim Bin(n,p_1)\), then for large \(n\),

\[ Z= \frac{Y_1-np_1} {\sqrt{np_1(1-p_1)}} \approx N(0,1) \]

So,

\[ Z^2 \approx \chi^2_1 \]

Pearson’s idea

Instead of only checking one binomial count, compare every observed count to its expected count.

For two categories:

\[ Q_1= \sum_{i=1}^{2} \frac{(Y_i-np_i)^2}{np_i} \]

This extends naturally to more than two categories.

10.1 Multinomial Response Model

A multinomial response model applies when each trial results in exactly one of \(k\) mutually exclusive and exhaustive categories.

The category probabilities are:

\[ p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_k \]

with

\[ p_1+p_2+\cdots+p_k=1 \]

Trials There are \(n\) independent and identical trials.

Categories Each trial lands in one of \(k\) possible categories.

Counts The observed category counts are \(Y_1,Y_2,\ldots,Y_k\).

Multinomial Counts

If the model probabilities are \(p_1,\ldots,p_k\), the expected count in category \(i\) is:

\[ E_i=np_i \]

The Pearson chi-square statistic is:

\[ Q_{k-1} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{(Y_i-np_i)^2}{np_i} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{(O_i-E_i)^2}{E_i} \]

For large enough expected counts,

\[ Q_{k-1}\approx \chi^2_{k-1} \]

The statistic gets large when observed counts are far from expected counts.

Example: Streaming App Preference

A campus media club asks \(n=240\) students which streaming app they would keep if they could keep only one.

The club wants to test whether the four apps are equally preferred.


App Netflix YouTube Disney+ Prime
Observed count 88 72 50 30

Use \(\alpha=0.05\).

Calculation

If all four apps are equally preferred:

\[ p_1=p_2=p_3=p_4=\frac14 \]

\[ E_i=np_i=240\left(\frac14\right)=60 \]

\[ \chi^2= \frac{(88-60)^2}{60} + \frac{(72-60)^2}{60} + \frac{(50-60)^2}{60} + \frac{(30-60)^2}{60} \]

\[ \chi^2=32.13,\qquad df=4-1=3 \]

Hypotheses

\[ H_0:p_1=p_2=p_3=p_4=\frac14 \]

\[ H_a:\text{the four probabilities are not all equal} \]

Since the P-value is very small, we reject \(H_0\). The sample gives strong evidence that the four streaming apps are not equally preferred.

Example: Campus Café Drink Preference

A campus café wants to know whether three cold drink options are equally preferred by students.

A random sample of \(n=180\) student purchases gives:


Drink Iced coffee Matcha latte Smoothie
Observed count 72 64 44

Use \(\alpha=0.05\).

Calculation

If all three drinks are equally preferred:

\[ p_1=p_2=p_3=\frac13 \]

\[ E_i=np_i=180\left(\frac13\right)=60 \]

\[ \chi^2= \frac{(72-60)^2}{60} + \frac{(64-60)^2}{60} + \frac{(44-60)^2}{60} \]

\[ \chi^2=6.93,\qquad df=3-1=2 \]

\[ p\text{-value}\approx0.031 \]

Hypotheses

\[ H_0:p_1=p_2=p_3=\frac13 \]

\[ H_a:\text{the three probabilities are not all equal} \]

Since \(0.031<0.05\), we reject \(H_0\). The sample suggests that the three café drinks are not equally preferred.

10.2 Chi-square Test for Goodness of Fit

A chi-square goodness-of-fit test checks whether observed category counts agree with a hypothesized probability model.

The hypotheses are:

\[ H_0: p_1=p_{hyp,1},\; p_2=p_{hyp,2},\; \ldots,\; p_k=p_{hyp,k} \]

\[ H_a:\text{at least one category probability is different} \]

The null hypothesis can say “all categories are equal,” but it can also give unequal hypothesized probabilities.

Goodness-of-Fit Test Statistic

The test statistic is:

\[ \chi^2 = \sum \frac{(\text{observed count}-\text{expected count})^2} {\text{expected count}} \]

where:

\[ \text{expected count}=np_i \]

The P-value is the area to the right of the observed \(\chi^2\) statistic under a chi-square distribution with:

\[ df=k-1 \]

This is always a right-tail test. Large \(\chi^2\) values mean the observed counts are too far from the hypothesized model.

Example: Campus Food Pickup Slots

A campus food hall predicts how mobile pickup orders are distributed across four time slots.

The historical model is:

\[ (0.15,\;0.35,\;0.30,\;0.20) \]

A random sample of \(n=200\) orders gives:

Slot 11-12 12-1 1-2 2-3
Observed count 24 82 54 40

Use \(\alpha=0.05\).

Calculation

Expected counts:

\[ 200(0.15,0.35,0.30,0.20) = (30,70,60,40) \]

\[ \chi^2= \frac{(24-30)^2}{30} + \frac{(82-70)^2}{70} + \frac{(54-60)^2}{60} + \frac{(40-40)^2}{40} \]

\[ \chi^2=3.86,\qquad df=4-1=3 \]

\[ p\text{-value}\approx0.277 \]

Hypotheses

\[ H_0:(p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4) = (0.15,0.35,0.30,0.20) \]

\[ H_a:\text{at least one probability differs} \]

Since \(0.277>0.05\), we do not reject \(H_0\). The observed pickup counts are reasonably consistent with the historical time-slot model.

Example: Phone Case Colour Demand

A phone accessory shop expects case colour demand to follow:

\[ (0.30,\;0.25,\;0.25,\;0.20) \]

for black, clear, blue, and pink cases.

A sample of \(n=150\) recent purchases gives:

Colour Black Clear Blue Pink
Observed count 63 28 36 23

Use \(\alpha=0.05\).

Calculation

Expected counts:

\[ 150(0.30,0.25,0.25,0.20) = (45,37.5,37.5,30) \]

\[ \chi^2= \frac{(63-45)^2}{45} + \frac{(28-37.5)^2}{37.5} + \frac{(36-37.5)^2}{37.5} + \frac{(23-30)^2}{30} \]

\[ \chi^2=11.30,\qquad df=3 \]

\[ p\text{-value}\approx0.0102 \]

Interpretation setup

One term of the statistic is especially large:

\[ \frac{(63-45)^2}{45}=7.20 \]

This shows that black case purchases are far above what the model expected.

Since \(0.0102<0.05\), we reject \(H_0\). The colour demand pattern does not fit the shop’s expected distribution well.

Reading the Chi-square Statistic

Each category contributes one term:

\[ \frac{(O_i-E_i)^2}{E_i} \]

The total \(\chi^2\) statistic is the sum of these terms.

How to read the terms

A category contributes more when the observed count is far from the expected count.

This helps explain why the final statistic is small or large.

10.3 Sample Size Assumption

The chi-square approximation works well only when the expected counts are large enough.

A common course rule is:

\[ E_i=np_i\ge 5 \quad \text{for every category} \]

Good situation

Expected counts:

\[ (30,70,60,40) \]

Every expected count is at least 5, so the chi-square approximation is reasonable.

Problem situation

Expected counts:

\[ (2.5,4.5,18,25) \]

Some expected counts are below 5, so the chi-square approximation may be unreliable.

Assumptions for Chi-square Goodness of Fit

Assumptions

  • Categorical counts: The data should be counts in mutually exclusive categories.
  • One category per trial: Each observation should fall into exactly one of the possible categories.
  • Independent trials: The trials or observations should be independent.
  • Expected cell frequency: Each expected count should usually be at least 5.

Course reminder

Check expected counts, not observed counts. A small observed count is not automatically a problem if the expected count condition is satisfied.

Question

A goodness-of-fit test has five categories. Under \(H_0\), the expected counts are:

\[ (8,\;12,\;20,\;4,\;16) \]

Which statement is best?

  • The test is fine because the total sample size is large
  • The test is fine because most observed counts are above 5
  • The chi-square approximation is questionable because one expected count is below 5
  • The degrees of freedom should be 5

Practice: Food Delivery App Share

A student group claims food delivery app choices on campus follow:

\[ (0.10,\;0.20,\;0.30,\;0.20,\;0.20) \]

A random sample of \(n=150\) students gives:

App A B C D E
Observed count 12 32 42 36 28

Test the model at the 1% significance level.

Calculation

Expected counts:

\[ 150(0.10,0.20,0.30,0.20,0.20) = (15,30,45,30,30) \]

\[ \chi^2= \frac{(12-15)^2}{15} + \frac{(32-30)^2}{30} + \frac{(42-45)^2}{45} + \frac{(36-30)^2}{30} + \frac{(28-30)^2}{30} \]

\[ \chi^2=2.27,\qquad df=5-1=4 \]

\[ p\text{-value}\approx0.687 \]

Decision rule

At the 1% level, reject \(H_0\) only if the P-value is less than \(0.01\).

Since \(0.687>0.01\), we do not reject \(H_0\). The observed app choices are consistent with the claimed distribution.

Chapter Summary

1. Model the counts Use a multinomial model when each trial falls into exactly one of \(k\) categories.

2. Build expected counts Under the null model:

\[ E_i=np_i \]

These are the counts we would expect if \(H_0\) were true.

3. Compare with \(\chi^2\) Use:

\[ \chi^2=\sum\frac{(O_i-E_i)^2}{E_i} \]

Large values give evidence against \(H_0\).


Degrees of freedom

\[ df=k-1 \]

P-value Use the right-tail area under the chi-square distribution.

Assumption check Each expected count should usually be at least 5.
