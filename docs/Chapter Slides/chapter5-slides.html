5.1 Point Estimates and Interval Estimates

Point estimate

A single numerical value computed from sample data that serves as the best guess of an unknown population parameter.

Examples: \(\bar{x}\) estimates \(\mu\), \(s^2\) estimates \(\sigma^2\), and \(\hat p\) estimates \(p\).

Confidence interval

A plausible range of values for a population parameter, built from sample data and a specified confidence level.

Big idea. A statistic will usually not equal the parameter exactly. The interval tells us how far away the statistic could reasonably be.

The CI Skeleton

Most confidence intervals (means, proportions, and their differences) follow the symmetric form:

\[ \text{estimate} \pm \underbrace{(\text{critical value})(\text{standard error})}_{\text{margin of error}} \]

Intervals for a variance \(\sigma^2\) follow their own skeleton — an asymmetric one built directly from the \(\chi^2\) distribution:

\[ \left( \frac{\text{estimate}}{\text{upper critical value}},\ \frac{\text{estimate}}{\text{lower critical value}} \right) \quad\text{where estimate} = (n-1)s^2 \]

Estimate \(\bar{x}\), \(\hat p\), \(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2\), or \(\hat p_1-\hat p_2\).

Critical value From \(Z\) or \(t\), depending on the sampling distribution.

Standard error The standard deviation of the estimator, or an estimate of it.

5.2 Interpreting Confidence

For a \(C\%\) confidence interval:

In repeated sampling, about \(C\%\) of intervals constructed using this method will capture the true parameter.

Do not say: “There is a 95% probability that this particular interval contains \(\mu\).”

The parameter is fixed. The random object is the interval produced by the sample.

CI Reference Guide

Target parameter Situation Reference distribution Interval shape
\(\mu\) one mean, \(\sigma\) known \(Z\) \(\bar{x}\pm z^*\sigma/\sqrt n\)
\(\mu\) one mean, \(\sigma\) unknown \(t_{n-1}\) \(\bar{x}\pm t^*s/\sqrt n\)
\(p\) one proportion \(Z\) \(\hat p\pm z^*\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\)
\(\sigma^2\) one variance, normal population \(\chi^2_{n-1}\) asymmetric
\(\mu_1-\mu_2\) two independent means \(Z\) or \(t\) difference \(\pm\) margin
\(p_1-p_2\) two independent proportions \(Z\) difference \(\pm\) margin
\(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_2^2\) ratio of variances \(F\) asymmetric
\(\mu_d\) paired data \(t_{n-1}\) \(\bar d\pm t^*s_d/\sqrt n\)

5.3.1.1 One Mean, Known \(\sigma\)

When the population standard deviation \(\sigma\) is known:

\[ \bar{x} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \]

Use this for a population mean \(\mu\) with known \(\sigma\).

Assumptions

  • Random sample or randomized experiment.
  • Independent observations.
  • Population is normal, or \(n\) is large enough for the CLT.

Exam cue If the problem gives a population standard deviation, use \(z\). If it gives a sample standard deviation, use \(t\).

Example: Daily Listening Time

Spotify reports a mean daily listening time of \(\bar{x}=92\) minutes from a random sample of \(n=80\) users. From years of logged data, the standard deviation is known to be \(\sigma=25\) minutes.

Construct 90%, 95%, and 99% confidence intervals for the population mean daily listening time.

Level Critical value Margin of error Confidence interval
90% 1.645 \(1.645(25/\sqrt{80})=4.60\) \((87.40, 96.60)\)
95% 1.960 \(1.960(25/\sqrt{80})=5.48\) \((86.52, 97.48)\)
99% 2.576 \(2.576(25/\sqrt{80})=7.20\) \((84.80, 99.20)\)

We are 95% confident that the mean daily listening time is between about 86.5 and 97.5 minutes.

Example: Active Minutes

A smartwatch logs daily active minutes (time spent moving) for a random sample of 15 people. Active minutes are normally distributed with known \(\sigma=15\) minutes:

\[79,43,58,66,101,63,79,33,58,71,60,101,74,55,88\]

Construct a 95% CI for \(\mu\).

\(\bar{x}=68.6\), so the interval is approximately \((61.01, 76.19)\). We are 95% confident that the population mean number of daily active minutes is between about 61 and 76.

5.3.1.2 One Mean, Unknown \(\sigma\)

When \(\sigma\) is unknown, estimate it with \(s\) and use the Student \(t\) distribution:

\[ \bar{x} \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\;n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt n} \]

Why t? Replacing \(\sigma\) with \(s\) adds uncertainty. The \(t\) distribution has heavier tails, especially for small degrees of freedom.

Small samples For small \(n\), check that the population is reasonably normal or that the sample has no severe skew/outliers.

Example: Hours of Sleep

Total hours of sleep logged over one week by a random sample of 9 students:

\[63.4,65.0,64.4,63.3,54.8,64.5,60.8,49.1,51.0\]

Construct a 90% CI for the mean weekly sleep.

Using \(n=9\), \(\bar{x}=59.5889\), \(s=6.2553\), and \(t^*=1.8595\):

\[59.5889\pm1.8595\frac{6.2553}{3}=(55.71,63.47).\]

Example: Books Read

A random sample of 10 readers reports how many books they finished this year:

\[25,6,22,26,31,18,13,20,14,2\]

Construct a 95% CI for the population mean number of books read.

\(\bar{x}=17.7\), \(s=9.0805\), \(df=9\), and \(t^*=2.262\). The 95% CI is approximately \((11.20,24.20)\) books.

5.3.2 One Proportion

For a population proportion \(p\), with \(\hat p=x/n\):

\[ \hat p \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}} \]

Conditions

  • Random sample.
  • Independent Bernoulli trials.
  • Sufficiently large counts: \(n\hat p \ge 10\) and \(n(1-\hat p)\ge 10\).

Boundary check The final interval should make sense for a proportion. Values below 0 or above 1 need careful handling.

Example: Transit Use

A UTM survey asks 1200 students whether they commute on MiWay transit. Among them, 738 say yes.

Construct a 95% CI for the true proportion of students who commute on MiWay.

\[\hat p=\frac{738}{1200}=0.6150\]

\[SE=\sqrt{\frac{0.6150(0.3850)}{1200}}=0.01405\]

\[\hat p \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,SE\]

\[=0.6150\pm1.96(0.01405)\]

\[=(0.5875,\,0.6425)\]

We are 95% confident that between 58.75% and 64.25% of UTM students commute on MiWay.

Example: All-Nighters

In a survey of 650 UTM students, 286 admit to pulling an all-nighter to finish an assignment this term.

Construct a 90% CI for the population proportion.

\(\hat p=0.44\), so the 90% CI is approximately \((0.4079,0.4721)\).

5.3.3 One Variance

For a normally distributed population, a confidence interval for \(\sigma^2\) is:

\[ \left( \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{1-\alpha/2,\;n-1}}, \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{\alpha/2,\;n-1}} \right) \]

This interval is asymmetric. Normality matters here much more than it does for large-sample mean intervals.

Example: Gym Hours

Exercise is proven to improve focus. The UTM gym logs the weekly workout hours of 25 students, with sample variance \(s^2=12.5\) (hours\(^2\)). Assume weekly hours are normally distributed.

Construct a 95% CI for \(\sigma^2\).

\[df=n-1=24\]

\[\chi^2_{0.975,24}=39.364\]

\[\chi^2_{0.025,24}=12.401\]

\[\left(\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{0.975,24}},\ \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{0.025,24}}\right)\]

\[=\left(\frac{24(12.5)}{39.364},\ \frac{24(12.5)}{12.401}\right)\]

\[=(7.62,\,24.19)\]

The 95% CI for the population variance is approximately \((7.62,24.19)\).

Example: Water Intake

Staying hydrated is linked to sharper concentration. A study records the daily water intake of 16 students, with sample variance \(s^2=0.36\) (litres\(^2\)). Assume daily intake is normally distributed.

Construct a 90% CI for \(\sigma^2\).

The 90% CI is approximately \((0.2160,0.7437)\).

5.4 Two-Sample Confidence Intervals

A two-sample confidence interval estimates a population quantity that compares two groups.

The groups must be clearly labelled, because the order matters. Changing the order changes the interpretation.

Mean and proportion differences

\[ \text{Group 1}-\text{Group 2} \]

Variance ratios

\[ \frac{\text{Group 1}}{\text{Group 2}} \]

Difference of means

\[ \mu_1-\mu_2 \]

Compares two population averages.

Difference of proportions

\[ p_1-p_2 \]

Compares two population percentages.

Ratio of variances

\[ \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_2^2} \]

Compares two population variances.

5.4.1 Confidence Interval for a Difference of Means

For two independent populations, we often want to estimate:

\[ \mu_1-\mu_2 \]

A natural statistic is:

\[ \bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2 \]

Case 1 \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\) known

Use a \(z\) interval.

Rare in practice.

Case 2 \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\) unknown, but assumed equal

Use a pooled two-sample \(t\) interval.

Case 3 \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\) unknown and assumed unequal

Use Welch’s two-sample \(t\) interval.

Difference of Means: Known \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\)

When the population standard deviations are known, a level \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\) is:

\[ (\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2) \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \sqrt{ \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2} } \]

This case is mathematically useful but rare in real applications, because population standard deviations are usually unknown.

Example: Hockey Shot-Speed Comparison

A sports analytics group compares slapshot speeds from a USA-Canada hockey rivalry skills event.

Long-term player-tracking data gives the population standard deviations.


Group 1: Team Canada players
\(n_1=64,\quad \bar{x}_1=94.8,\quad \sigma_1=8\)

Group 2: Team USA players
\(n_2=81,\quad \bar{x}_2=91.6,\quad \sigma_2=7\)

Construct a 95% CI for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\).

Calculation

\[ \bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2 = 94.8-91.6 = 3.2 \]

\[ SE= \sqrt{ \frac{8^2}{64} + \frac{7^2}{81} } = \sqrt{1.0000+0.6049} = 1.2669 \]

\[ 3.2\pm1.96(1.2669) = 3.2\pm2.48 \]

\[ (0.72,\;5.68) \]

Target parameter

We are estimating \(\mu_1-\mu_2\), where:

\(\mu_1=\) mean slapshot speed for Team Canada players

\(\mu_2=\) mean slapshot speed for Team USA players

The order is Canada minus USA.

We are 95% confident that Team Canada’s population mean slapshot speed is between 0.72 and 5.68 mph higher than Team USA’s population mean slapshot speed.

Difference of Means: Equal Unknown Variances

If \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\) are unknown but assumed equal, we pool the two sample variances.

The pooled variance is:

\[ s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2} \]

The level \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\) is:

\[ (\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2) \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\;n_1+n_2-2} s_p \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}} \]

Use this only when the equal-variance assumption is reasonable. Otherwise, use Welch’s interval.

Example: Earbud Battery-Life Comparison

A tech reviewer wants to compare average battery life for two wireless earbud brands that students might use while commuting, studying, or working out.

Each pair is tested under the same conditions: same volume, same audio, and same room temperature.

Assume the two population variances are reasonable to treat as equal.


Group 1: Brand A earbuds
\(n_1=12\), \(\bar{x}_1=52.4\) hours, \(s_1=6.1\)

Group 2: Brand B earbuds
\(n_2=10\), \(\bar{x}_2=47.8\) hours, \(s_2=5.4\)

Calculation

\[ {\large \bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2=52.4-47.8=4.6} \]

\[ {\large s_p^2= \frac{(11)(6.1^2)+(9)(5.4^2)}{20} =33.588, \qquad s_p=5.795 } \]

\[ {\large SE= 5.795\sqrt{\frac{1}{12}+\frac{1}{10}} =2.481 } \]

\[ {\large df=20, \qquad t^*=2.086 } \]

\[ {\large 4.6\pm2.086(2.481)=(-0.58,\;9.78) } \]

Target parameter

Construct a 95% confidence interval for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\).

The order is Brand A minus Brand B, so positive values mean Brand A has the higher population mean battery life.

We are 95% confident that Brand A’s population mean battery life is between 0.58 hours lower and 9.78 hours higher than Brand B’s population mean battery life.

Example: Fitness App Calorie-Burn Comparison

A fitness reviewer wants to compare average calories burned during two types of 30-minute home workouts.

One group follows a high-intensity workout app. The other group follows a lower-intensity guided stretching and strength routine.

Assume the two population variances are reasonable to treat as equal.


Group 1: High-intensity app workout
\(n_1=15\), \(\bar{x}_1=84.2\) calories, \(s_1=7.5\)

Group 2: Guided strength/stretch routine
\(n_2=14\), \(\bar{x}_2=78.6\) calories, \(s_2=6.8\)

Calculation

\[ {\large \bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2=84.2-78.6=5.6} \]

\[ {\large s_p^2= \frac{(14)(7.5^2)+(13)(6.8^2)}{27} =51.430, \qquad s_p=7.171 } \]

\[ {\large SE= 7.171\sqrt{\frac{1}{15}+\frac{1}{14}} =2.665 } \]

\[ {\large df=27, \qquad t^*=1.703 } \]

\[ {\large 5.6\pm1.703(2.665)=(1.06,\;10.14) } \]

Target parameter

Construct a 90% confidence interval for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\).

The order is high-intensity app workout minus guided routine, so positive values mean the high-intensity app workout has the higher population mean calories burned.

We are 90% confident that the high-intensity app workout burns between 1.06 and 10.14 more calories, on average, than the guided strength/stretch routine.

Difference of Means: Unequal Unknown Variances

If the population variances are unknown and are assumed unequal, use Welch’s two-sample \(t\) interval:

\[ (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,df} \sqrt{ \frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2} } \]

This estimates:

\[ \mu_1 - \mu_2 \]

when the two samples are independent and the equal-variance assumption is not reasonable.

Course note. Welch’s method is the more flexible default when the two sample variances look meaningfully different.

Welch Degrees of Freedom

Degrees of freedom note

In this course, we are not testing your ability to be a calculator in an exam setting.

For hand calculations, you may use the conservative degrees of freedom:

\[ df = \min(n_1 - 1,\; n_2 - 1) \]

A more accurate approximation, commonly used by software, is the Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom:

\[ df = \frac{ \left(\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2 }{ \frac{\left(s_1^2/n_1\right)^2}{n_1-1} + \frac{\left(s_2^2/n_2\right)^2}{n_2-1} } \]

For exams in this course, use the conservative version unless told otherwise.

Example: Sports Highlight Watch-Time Comparison

A sports media team compares how long viewers spend watching two types of highlight playlists.

One playlist has shorter, fast-paced clips, while the other has longer, more detailed highlight clips.


Group 1: Short-highlight viewers
\(n_1=24,\quad \bar{x}_1=9.8,\quad s_1=3.1\) minutes

Group 2: Long-highlight viewers
\(n_2=20,\quad \bar{x}_2=12.6,\quad s_2=5.8\) minutes

Construct a 95% CI for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\) using Welch’s method.

Calculation

\[ \bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2 = 9.8-12.6 = -2.8 \]

\[ SE= \sqrt{ \frac{3.1^2}{24} + \frac{5.8^2}{20} } = \sqrt{0.4004+1.6820} = 1.4431 \]

Using conservative degrees of freedom:

\[ df=\min(24-1,\;20-1)=19 \]

\[ -2.8\pm2.093(1.4431) = -2.8\pm3.02 \]

\[ (-5.82,\;0.22) \]

Target parameter

We are estimating \(\mu_1-\mu_2\), where:

\(\mu_1=\) mean watch time for short-highlight viewers

\(\mu_2=\) mean watch time for long-highlight viewers

The order is short highlights minus long highlights.

We are 95% confident that the short-highlight playlist’s population mean watch time is between 5.82 minutes lower and 0.22 minutes higher than the long-highlight playlist’s population mean watch time. Since the interval crosses 0, the data do not show a clear difference in mean watch time.

Example: Food Delivery vs Meal Prep Spending

A budgeting app wants to compare average weekly food spending for two types of young adults.

One group often orders from food delivery apps. The other group usually meal preps or cooks at home.

The sample standard deviations are noticeably different, so equal population variances are not assumed.


Group 1: Food delivery users
\(n_1=18\), \(\bar{x}_1=96\) dollars, \(s_1=22\)

Group 2: Meal prep users
\(n_2=22\), \(\bar{x}_2=82\) dollars, \(s_2=16\)

Calculation

\[ \bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2=96-82=14 \]

\[ SE= \sqrt{ \frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2} } = \sqrt{ \frac{22^2}{18} + \frac{16^2}{22} } = 6.207 \]

\[ df\approx 30.3, \qquad t^*\approx1.697 \]

\[ \begin{aligned} (\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2)\pm t^*SE &= 14\pm1.697(6.207) \\ &= (3.47,\;24.53) \end{aligned} \]

Target parameter

Construct a 90% confidence interval for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\).

The order is food delivery users minus meal prep users, so positive values mean food delivery users have the higher population mean weekly food spending.

We are 90% confident that food delivery users spend between 3.47 dollars and 24.53 dollars more per week on food, on average, than meal prep users.

Assumptions for Two-Sample Mean Intervals

Assumptions

  • The two chosen samples should be independent and randomly selected, or come from a randomized experiment.
  • If both sample sizes are small, \(n_1<30\) and \(n_2<30\), then both populations should be approximately normal.
  • If only one sample size is small, then the smaller-sample population should be approximately normal.

CLT note

If both \(n_1\ge 30\) and \(n_2\ge 30\), the normality assumption is not required by the Central Limit Theorem.

5.4.2 Confidence Interval for a Difference of Proportions

For two independent samples, let:

\[ \hat{p}_1=\frac{x_1}{n_1}, \qquad \hat{p}_2=\frac{x_2}{n_2} \]

A level \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(p_1-p_2\) is:

\[ (\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2) \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \sqrt{ \frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2} } \]

The interval estimates the difference between two population proportions, not the difference between two sample counts.

Example: Free-Trial Reminder Comparison

A streaming app wants to compare two ways of reminding users before a free trial ends.

One group receives a direct reminder about the upcoming charge. The other group receives a general app notification.


Group 1: Direct reminder users
\(x_1=78\) of \(n_1=300\) continued the subscription

Group 2: General notification users
\(x_2=52\) of \(n_2=280\) continued the subscription

Calculation

\[ \hat p_1=\frac{78}{300}=0.2600, \qquad \hat p_2=\frac{52}{280}=0.1857 \]

\[ \hat p_1-\hat p_2 = 0.2600-0.1857 = 0.0743 \]

\[ SE= \sqrt{ \frac{0.2600(0.7400)}{300} + \frac{0.1857(0.8143)}{280} } = 0.0344 \]

\[ 0.0743\pm1.96(0.0344) = (0.0069,\;0.1417) \]

Target parameter

Construct a 95% confidence interval for \(p_1-p_2\).

The order is direct reminder minus general notification, so positive values mean the direct reminder has the higher population continuation rate.

We are 95% confident that the direct reminder’s population continuation rate is between 0.69 and 14.17 percentage points higher than the general notification’s population continuation rate.

Example: App Notification Click-Through Comparison

A mobile app wants to compare two ways of encouraging users to open a new feature.

One group receives a direct push notification about the feature. The other group only sees a generic homepage banner.


Group 1: Push notification users
\(x_1=210\) of \(n_1=500\) opened the feature within 24 hours

Group 2: Homepage banner users
\(x_2=170\) of \(n_2=450\) opened the feature within 24 hours

Calculation

\[ \hat p_1=\frac{210}{500}=0.4200, \qquad \hat p_2=\frac{170}{450}=0.3778 \]

\[ \hat p_1-\hat p_2=0.4200-0.3778=0.0422 \]

\[ SE= \sqrt{ \frac{0.4200(0.5800)}{500} + \frac{0.3778(0.6222)}{450} } = 0.0318 \]

\[ 0.0422\pm1.645(0.0318) = (-0.0100,\;0.0945) \]

Target parameter

Construct a 90% confidence interval for \(p_1-p_2\).

The order is push notification minus homepage banner, so positive values mean the push notification has the higher population proportion of users who open the feature.

We are 90% confident that the push notification’s population proportion is between 1.00 percentage point lower and 9.45 percentage points higher than the homepage banner’s population proportion.

Assumptions for Difference of Proportions

Assumptions

  • Randomization condition: The data in each group should be drawn independently and at random from a population, or generated by a completely randomized designed experiment.
  • 10% condition: If the data are sampled without replacement, each sample should not exceed 10% of the target population. If a sample is bigger than 10% of the target population, random draws are no longer approximately independent.
  • Independent groups assumption: The two groups being compared must be independent from each other.
  • Large-count condition: Each group should have at least 10 observed successes and at least 10 observed failures: \(n_1\hat p_1 \ge 10\), \(n_1(1-\hat p_1)\ge 10\), \(n_2\hat p_2 \ge 10\), and \(n_2(1-\hat p_2)\ge 10\).

Practical reminder

Before interpreting the interval, clearly state the order of the groups. The difference \(p_1-p_2\) has the opposite interpretation from \(p_2-p_1\).

5.4.3 Confidence Interval for a Ratio of Variances

To compare two population variances, the parameter is:

\[ \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_2^2} \]

If both populations are normal and the samples are independent, then:

\[ \frac{s_1^2/\sigma_1^2}{s_2^2/\sigma_2^2} \sim F_{n_1-1,\;n_2-1} \]

A ratio near 1 suggests similar variances. A ratio far above or below 1 suggests one population is more variable.

Ratio of Variances: Formula

A level \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_2^2\) is:

\[ \left( \frac{s_1^2/s_2^2}{F_{1-\alpha/2,\;n_1-1,\;n_2-1}}, \; \frac{s_1^2/s_2^2}{F_{\alpha/2,\;n_1-1,\;n_2-1}} \right) \]

This interval is sensitive to the normality assumption. For variance ratios, normality matters more than it does for large-sample mean intervals.

Example: Sale Checkout-Time Variability

An online retailer wants to compare how inconsistent checkout times are during a normal purchase period versus a limited-time sale period.


Group 1: Regular checkout period
\(n_1=18,\quad s_1=6\) minutes

Group 2: Limited-time sale checkout period
\(n_2=16,\quad s_2=14\) minutes

Construct a 95% confidence interval for:

\[ \frac{\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2} \]

Calculation

\[ \frac{s_2^2}{s_1^2} = \frac{14^2}{6^2} = 5.444 \]

\[ F_{0.975,15,17}=2.723, \qquad F_{0.025,15,17}=0.356 \]

\[ \left( \frac{5.444}{2.723}, \frac{5.444}{0.356} \right) = (2.00,\;15.31) \]

Target parameter

The order is limited-time sale checkout variability divided by regular checkout variability.

Values above 1 mean the limited-time sale checkout times are more variable.

We are 95% confident that the limited-time sale checkout-time variance is between 2.00 and 15.31 times the regular checkout-time variance. This suggests the sale checkout process is much less consistent.

Example: Charging-Time Variability

A phone reviewer wants to compare how consistent two charging setups are.

Both setups are tested from 20% to 80% battery under similar conditions. The question is not which setup is faster on average, but which setup has more variability in charging time.

Assume charging times are approximately normal.


Group 1: Budget fast charger
\(n_1=16\), \(s_1=12\) minutes

Group 2: Branded fast charger
\(n_2=14\), \(s_2=8\) minutes

Calculation

\[ \frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2} = \frac{12^2}{8^2} = 2.25 \]

\[ df_1=n_1-1=15, \qquad df_2=n_2-1=13 \]

\[ F_{0.975,15,13}=3.053, \qquad F_{0.025,15,13}=0.342 \]

\[ \left( \frac{2.25}{3.053}, \frac{2.25}{0.342} \right) = (0.74,\;6.58) \]

Target parameter

Construct a 95% confidence interval for \(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_2^2\).

The order is budget charger variance divided by branded charger variance, so values above 1 mean the budget charger has more variable charging times.

We are 95% confident that the budget charger’s population variance in charging time is between 0.74 and 6.58 times the branded charger’s population variance. Since 1 is inside the interval, equal variances are plausible.

Assumptions for Ratio of Variances

Assumptions

  • Independence: The two samples must be independent of each other.
  • Random sampling: Each sample should be obtained through a random selection process.
  • Normality: Both populations should be normally distributed. This is important because the ratio of sample variances follows an \(F\) distribution only when the underlying populations are normal.
  • Equal measurement scale: The two variables being compared should be measured on the same or comparable scales.

Important note

Violations of these assumptions, especially normality, can lead to inaccurate confidence intervals and invalid conclusions.

5.5 Confidence Intervals on Paired Data

Paired data occur when observations are naturally matched.

Instead of comparing two independent sample means, we compute differences:

\[ d_i = x_{2i} - x_{1i} \]

Then we build a one-sample \(t\) interval for the population mean difference \(\mu_d\).

Few Examples

  • Before and after measurements on the same person
  • Matched twins
  • Same student under two testing conditions
  • Same object measured by two devices

Do not treat paired data as two independent samples. The pairing is the main information.

Paired Data: Formula

Let \(d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_n\) be the paired differences.

The level \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu_d\) is:

\[ \bar{d} \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\;n-1} \frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}} \]

where \(\bar{d}\) is the sample mean of the differences and \(s_d\) is the sample standard deviation of the differences.

The normality condition applies to the differences, not necessarily to the original two measurements separately.

Each grey line connects the same person from before to after. The coloured dots identify the paired observations, and the labels show the within-person increase.

Example: Vitamin C and Sick Days

A researcher wants to study whether regular Vitamin C use is associated with fewer sick days during cold season.

The same 12 people are observed across two comparable winter periods: one without regular Vitamin C use and one with regular Vitamin C use.

For each person, define:

\[ d=\text{sick days without Vitamin C}-\text{sick days with Vitamin C} \]

Observed differences:

\[ 2,\;1,\;3,\;0,\;2,\;4,\;1,\;2,\;3,\;1,\;0,\;2 \]

Calculation

\[ n=12, \qquad \bar d=1.750, \qquad s_d=1.215 \]

\[ SE= \frac{s_d}{\sqrt n} = \frac{1.215}{\sqrt{12}} = 0.351 \]

\[ df=n-1=11, \qquad t^*=2.201 \]

\[ \bar d\pm t^*SE = 1.750\pm2.201(0.351) = (0.98,\;2.52) \]

Target parameter

Construct a 95% confidence interval for \(\mu_d\).

The order is without Vitamin C minus with Vitamin C, so positive values mean fewer sick days during the Vitamin C period.

We are 95% confident that regular Vitamin C use is associated with between 0.98 and 2.52 fewer sick days, on average, for people like those in this paired study.

Example: Phone-Free Bedtime Routine

A sleep app wants to study whether a phone-free bedtime routine is associated with more sleep.

The same 12 people track their sleep before and after using a 30-minute phone-free routine before bed.

For each person, define:

\[ d=\text{sleep after routine}-\text{sleep before routine} \]

Observed differences in minutes:

\[ 18,\;12,\;24,\;9,\;30,\;15,\;21,\;6,\;27,\;12,\;18,\;24 \]

Calculation

\[ n=12, \qquad \bar d=18.000, \qquad s_d=7.459 \]

\[ SE= \frac{s_d}{\sqrt n} = \frac{7.459}{\sqrt{12}} = 2.153 \]

\[ df=n-1=11, \qquad t^*=1.796 \]

\[ \bar d\pm t^*SE = 18.000\pm1.796(2.153) = (14.13,\;21.87) \]

Target parameter

Construct a 90% confidence interval for \(\mu_d\).

The order is after routine minus before routine, so positive values mean more sleep after using the phone-free bedtime routine.

We are 90% confident that the phone-free bedtime routine is associated with between 14.13 and 21.87 more minutes of sleep, on average, for people like those in this paired study.

Assumptions for Paired Data

Assumptions

  • Paired observations: Each pair of values should come from the same unit or from meaningfully matched units.
  • Independence of pairs: The pairs themselves should be independent of each other.
  • Normality of differences: The distribution of the paired differences should be approximately normal, especially for small samples.
  • Random sampling: The sample of pairs should be obtained through a random process or a randomized design.

Important note

The normality condition applies to the differences, not to the original two measurements separately. For larger samples, this condition becomes less critical because of the Central Limit Theorem.

5.6 Sample Size Selection Using Confidence Intervals

Sample size selection works backwards.

Instead of asking:

What margin of error do we get from this sample size?

we ask:

What sample size do we need to achieve this margin of error?

For a mean

The required sample size depends on:

  • confidence level
  • desired margin of error
  • population standard deviation, or an estimate of it

For a proportion

The required sample size depends on:

  • confidence level
  • desired margin of error
  • a planning value \(p_0\)

If no reliable prior estimate is available, use \(p_0=0.5\) for the most conservative sample size.

5.6.1 Sample Size for a Mean When \(\sigma\) is Known

For a confidence interval on a population mean with known \(\sigma\):

\[ E = z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]

Solving for \(n\) gives:

\[ n = \left( \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}\sigma}{E} \right)^2 \]

Always round up to the next whole number. Rounding down would make the margin of error too large.

Example: Online Store Waitlist Survey

An online store is planning a limited product release for customers on a waitlist.

Before the release, the company wants to estimate the proportion of waitlist users who plan to make a purchase when it goes live.

A previous customer survey suggests a planning value of:

\[ p_0=0.156 \]

The company wants the margin of error to be at most:

\[ E=0.03 \]

Find the required sample size for 95% and 99% confidence.

Calculation

\[ n= \left( \frac{z^*}{E} \right)^2 p_0(1-p_0) \]

\[ p_0=0.156, \qquad 1-p_0=0.844, \qquad E=0.03 \]

\[ n_{95}= \left( \frac{1.96}{0.03} \right)^2 (0.156)(0.844) = 562.0005 \Rightarrow 563 \]

\[ n_{99}= \left( \frac{2.576}{0.03} \right)^2 (0.156)(0.844) = 970.7698 \Rightarrow 971 \]

Planning goal

Find the sample size needed for a confidence interval for the population proportion of waitlist users who plan to make a purchase.

The margin of error should be no more than 3 percentage points.

The company should survey at least 563 waitlist users for 95% confidence and 971 waitlist users for 99% confidence. Higher confidence requires a larger sample size when the margin of error stays fixed.

Example: Pickup-Time Estimate Accuracy

A ride-share app wants to estimate its average pickup-time prediction error.

From past trip data, the population standard deviation of prediction error is treated as known:

\[ \sigma = 22.5 \text{ seconds} \]

The app wants the margin of error to be at most:

\[ E = 2 \text{ seconds} \]

Find the required sample size for 99% confidence.

Calculation

\[ n= \left( \frac{z^*\sigma}{E} \right)^2 \]

\[ z^*=2.576, \qquad \sigma=22.5, \qquad E=2 \]

\[ n= \left( \frac{2.576(22.5)}{2} \right)^2 \]

\[ n=839.8404 \]

\[ n=840 \]

Planning goal

Choose \(n\) so the confidence interval for the population mean prediction error has margin of error no more than 2 seconds.

The app should sample at least 840 trips to estimate the mean pickup-time prediction error within 2 seconds at 99% confidence.

Sample Size for a Mean When \(\sigma\) is Unknown

When \(\sigma\) is unknown, we use a preliminary estimate \(s\) from a pilot study or previous data.

A practical formula is:

\[ n = \left( \frac{t_{1-\alpha/2,\;n-1}s}{E} \right)^2 \]

but the \(t\) critical value depends on \(n\), the quantity we are trying to find.

This is why an iterative approach is used:

  1. Start with the \(z\) approximation.
  2. Use that \(n\) to get a \(t\) critical value.
  3. Recalculate \(n\).
  4. Repeat until \(n\) stabilizes.
  5. Round up.

Example: Tutorial Video Rating Estimate

An online learning platform wants to estimate the mean rating students give to a new tutorial video.

Each student rates the video on a 0 to 100 scale.

Since \(\sigma\) is unknown, the platform uses a pilot study:

\[ s=14.5 \]

They want the margin of error to be at most:

\[ E=4 \]

Find the required sample size for 95% confidence.

Calculation

\[ n= \left( \frac{t^*s}{E} \right)^2 \]

Start with the \(z\) approximation:

\[ n_0= \left( \frac{1.96(14.5)}{4} \right)^2 = 50.48 \Rightarrow 51 \]

Then update using \(t\) critical values:

\[ \begin{array}{c|c|c|c} \text{Use } n & df & t^* & \text{New } n \\ \hline 51 & 50 & 2.009 & 53.04 \Rightarrow 54 \\ 54 & 53 & 2.006 & 52.88 \Rightarrow 53 \\ 53 & 52 & 2.007 & 52.94 \Rightarrow 53 \end{array} \]

So the sample size stabilizes at:

\[ n=53 \]

Planning goal

We are planning a confidence interval for the population mean student rating.

Because \(\sigma\) is unknown, the pilot-study standard deviation \(s=14.5\) is used as the preliminary estimate.

The online learning platform should sample at least 53 students to estimate the mean tutorial-video rating within 4 points at 95% confidence.

Example: Grocery-Delivery Fee Estimate

A consumer app wants to estimate the average extra cost from grocery delivery fees, service charges, and tips.

A pilot sample gives an estimated standard deviation of:

\[ s=16\text{ dollars} \]

The app wants a 95% confidence interval with margin of error at most:

\[ E=10\text{ dollars} \]

Since \(\sigma\) is unknown, use the iterative \(t\) approach.

Calculation

Start with the \(z\) approximation:

\[ n_0= \left\lceil \left(\frac{1.960(16)}{10}\right)^2 \right\rceil = 10 \]

Then update using \(t\) critical values:

\[ \begin{array}{c|c|c|c} \text{Use } n & df & t^* & \text{New } n \\ \hline 10 & 9 & 2.262 & 13.10 \Rightarrow 14 \\ 14 & 13 & 2.160 & 11.94 \Rightarrow 12 \\ 12 & 11 & 2.201 & 12.40 \Rightarrow 13 \\ 13 & 12 & 2.179 & 12.16 \Rightarrow 13 \end{array} \]

So the sample size stabilizes at:

\[ n=13 \]

Planning goal

Find the sample size needed for a 95% confidence interval for the population mean extra grocery-delivery cost.

Because \(\sigma\) is unknown, the pilot estimate \(s=16\) is used.

The iteration stabilizes at 13, so the app should sample at least 13 grocery-delivery orders to keep the margin of error at most 10 dollars.

5.6.2 Sample Size for a Proportion

For a confidence interval on a population proportion, the margin of error is approximately:

\[ E = z_{1-\alpha/2} \sqrt{ \frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n} } \]

Solving for \(n\) gives:

\[ n = \left( \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{E} \right)^2 p_0(1-p_0) \]

The value \(p_0\) is a planning value. If no prior information is available, use \(p_0=0.5\) because it gives the most conservative sample size.

Example: Ad-Supported Plan Survey

A streaming service wants to estimate the proportion of subscribers who would switch to a cheaper ad-supported plan.

The company wants a 95% confidence interval with margin of error no more than:

\[ E=0.03 \]

There is no reliable prior estimate for the true proportion, so use the conservative planning value:

\[ p_0=0.5 \]

Calculation

\[ n= \left( \frac{z^*}{E} \right)^2 p_0(1-p_0) \]

\[ z^*=1.96, \qquad E=0.03, \qquad p_0=0.5 \]

\[ n= \left( \frac{1.96}{0.03} \right)^2 (0.5)(0.5) \]

\[ n=1067.11 \]

\[ n=1068 \]

Planning goal

Find the sample size needed for a 95% confidence interval for a population proportion.

The margin of error should be no more than 3 percentage points.

The streaming service should survey at least 1068 subscribers. We round up because rounding down would make the margin of error slightly larger than 3 percentage points.

Chapter Summary

Choose the correct interval

  • Identify the population parameter.
  • Decide whether the data involve one sample, two independent samples, or paired observations.
  • Determine whether the target is a mean, proportion, variance, or difference between parameters.
  • Select the appropriate reference distribution: \(Z\), \(t\), \(\chi^2\), or \(F\).

Report the result correctly

  • State the confidence level.
  • Include units when relevant.
  • Interpret the interval in the context of the population parameter.
  • Explain the meaning of 0 or 1 when interpreting differences or ratios.
  • Always round a required sample size up.


One-slide takeaway. A confidence interval combines a sample estimate with a measure of uncertainty to give a plausible range of values for an unknown population parameter.
